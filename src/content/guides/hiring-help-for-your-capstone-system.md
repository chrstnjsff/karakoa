Plenty of students get outside help with their capstone system, and schools expect some of it.
Advisers, senior students, online tutorials, and professional developers all play a part.
The line that matters is simple: on defense day you must be able to explain and operate the system yourself.

## What is normally acceptable

- Consultation on scope, architecture, stack, and database design.
- Help building the system, as long as you take part and can explain every module.
- Code review and debugging of code you wrote.
- Setting up hosting, deployment, and test data.
- Support for the technical sections of the manuscript, such as diagrams and test plans, that describe the real system.
- A mock defense and a walkthrough of the code before the real one.

What is not acceptable anywhere is submitting a system or a manuscript you did not take part in and cannot explain.
Your capstone manual will have its own rules on authorship and academic honesty, so read it and follow it.

## Questions to ask before you pay anyone

- Will you walk me through the code, module by module, until I can explain it to a panel?
- Can we use the stack my school requires?
- Who owns the source code at the end? The answer should be you, with the full repository handed over.
- How are revisions after the proposal or final defense handled, and for how long?
- What is the milestone plan, and what do I see at each milestone?
- What are the payment terms? Expect a deposit, milestone payments, and a final payment on handover.
- What happens if the panel asks for a change you did not plan for?

A developer who cannot answer these clearly is not the right one.

## Red flags

- A finished system offered in days for a tiny amount. It is usually a renamed copy of a free project, and plagiarism checks and panel questions will expose it.
- No walkthrough offered, or an extra fee just to explain the code.
- Refusal to hand over the full source code and database schema.
- No written scope. Verbal agreements fall apart at the first revision request.
- Promises about grades or panel outcomes. Nobody can promise those.

## How to stay in control

- Keep the repository under your own account and give the developer access, not the other way around.
- Join the build sessions. Even watching the database schema take shape teaches you what the panel will ask.
- Write the objectives and scope yourself, then hand them to the developer as the brief.
- Test every feature yourself against the objectives before you accept a milestone.
- Explain the top five functions out loud to a teammate before the defense.
